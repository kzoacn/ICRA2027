\begin{abstract}
Language-conditioned manipulation requires semantic grounding, geometric
reasoning, and reliable transitions between physical interactions.
\method{} uses a frozen \NeuralMillion{}M-parameter model to detect objects
and geometric rules to control robot actions. It requires no robot
demonstrations for action-policy training. Each execution step can be
inspected, making failures easier to locate and fix.
The system compiles supported instructions into grasp--place and contact
skills using two calibrated RGB-D views and public asset priors. Explicit
state tracks the carried object, and local checks manage release, articulation,
and recovery. Benchmark success is recorded externally and is unavailable
to the policy.

\section{Introduction}
Natural-language instructions provide a compact description of a manipulation
goal, but leave much of the physical procedure unspecified. To place a bowl
inside a drawer, a robot must identify the intended object, infer accessible
geometry, establish a stable grasp, maintain clearance during transport, and
release the object in a suitable region. Articulation introduces an additional
difficulty: the robot must distinguish productive motion from mechanical
resistance and decide when to release or reacquire contact. A plausible
semantic interpretation alone does not resolve these questions.

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies offer one route to this capability.
OpenVLA~\cite{openvla} adapts pretrained representations to action prediction,
while SmolVLA~\cite{smolvla} studies a more compact architecture and efficient
execution. Both learn robot actions from demonstrations. We study a system
that uses pretrained perception to identify objects and computes actions from
their geometry. This design avoids training an action network and makes the
targets and transition checks of each skill available for inspection.
Its requirements include calibrated depth, asset knowledge, and manually
designed skills; these provide information that a learned policy may instead
acquire from robot data.

We present \method{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}), an asset-informed baseline for language-conditioned
tabletop manipulation. The system compiles supported instructions into
ordered skill programs and binds their entities to geometry reconstructed
from two calibrated RGB-D views. Its only neural model is a frozen
\NeuralMillion{}M-parameter detector, used together with a gallery derived
from public simulator assets. A dispatcher preserves state
across grasp--place segments and invokes specialized contact controllers for
drawers, doors, knobs, and pushing. Robot actions are bounded Cartesian pose
increments with a gripper command.

The central engineering issue is continuity across physical stages. A
semantically correct target can still be unreachable with the current wrist
orientation; a successful close command need not establish retention; and a
gripper placed at a receptacle center need not center the object being carried.
\method{} therefore maintains explicit object-to-gripper displacement,
re-observes relevant geometry at selected transitions, and uses local
completion checks before advancing its program. These checks are fallible
estimates, separate from benchmark success.

We evaluate the deployed implementation on the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long
suites of LIBERO~\cite{libero}. Each suite contains ten evaluated tasks, with
ten official initial states per task. The evaluation retains fixed task configurations and records controller
versions; its scope is a system study on
known benchmark assets and instruction forms. We make three contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A language-to-geometry manipulation system that requires no robot
      demonstrations for action-policy training, with its sensing, asset
      priors, and manually designed skills explicitly specified.
\item A compact deployment using one frozen \NeuralMillion{}M-parameter
      detector, with model-size and task-outcome comparisons to representative
      VLAs on the four LIBERO suites.
\item Inspectable skill execution with explicit geometric targets and local
      checks, illustrated by targeted revisions to microwave and drawer
      execution that improve Long 09 and Goal 03 without updating neural weights.
\end{enumerate}

The contribution is the integrated system and its empirical characterization.
The targeted revisions are development results; their records and those
of the original full evaluation retain their controller versions.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Language, Programs, and Geometric Planning}
Task and motion planning combines discrete decisions with continuous geometric
feasibility~\cite{tamp}. Language-conditioned systems extend this interface:
SayCan connects language choices to available skills and their affordances
\cite{saycan}, while Code as Policies generates executable programs that call
perception and control interfaces~\cite{codepolicies}. \method{} uses a
deterministic grammar and a fixed skill vocabulary. This makes the represented
program inspectable, but restricts language coverage to implemented
constructions and validated entities.

VoxPoser constructs spatial value maps to guide motion~\cite{voxposer}.
ReKep expresses manipulation objectives through relational keypoint
constraints~\cite{rekep}. These works show the utility of intermediate
geometric representations. Our implementation uses object and fixture
geometry, carried-object offsets, and specialized finite-state controllers.
It does not synthesize arbitrary constraint programs or perform a unified
optimization over the complete task. Its behavior depends on the coverage
and calibration of its manually implemented skills.

\subsection{Pretrained Perception and Action Policies}
Grounding DINO supports text-conditioned object detection~\cite{groundingdino}.
\method{} uses a frozen local Tiny checkpoint as one source of region proposals;
the downstream geometric and gallery components remain essential to the
implemented pipeline. In particular, public object textures and collision
dimensions provide priors that are stronger than an image-only input setting.

OpenVLA~\cite{openvla} and SmolVLA~\cite{smolvla} learn action generation from
robot data. OpenVLA-OFT~\cite{openvlaoft} studies action chunking, parallel
decoding, continuous action prediction, and additional sensor inputs.
\method{} provides a complementary, explicitly engineered control
baseline. The distinction concerns how actions are obtained; it does not imply
an absence of learning in the pretrained detector or an absence of development
on benchmark scenarios. Section~\ref{sec:comparison} compares model size,
policy-training requirements, and reported success rates while identifying
differences in sensing, asset access, and evaluation conditions.

\subsection{Benchmark Interpretation}
LIBERO was introduced to study knowledge transfer in lifelong robot
learning~\cite{libero}. We use its four commonly evaluated ten-task suites to
characterize a fixed manipulation system, rather than measuring continual
learning or forgetting. The choice of ten trials per task follows the sample
count used in SmolVLA's simulation study~\cite{smolvla}. Matching that count
alone does not reproduce its training or evaluation conditions. We specify
the remaining conditions in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.



\section{System}
\subsection{Observation and Prior Information}
At step $t$, the policy receives
\begin{equation}
 o_t=\left(\{I_t^c,D_t^c,K^c,T_{WC,t}^c\}_{c\in\{a,w\}},r_t\right),
\end{equation}
where $a$ and $w$ denote the fixed and wrist cameras, $I$ is RGB,
$D$ is metric depth, $K$ is calibration, and $T_{WC}$ maps camera
coordinates into the world frame. Robot measurements $r_t$ include
end-effector pose, joint state, gripper state, and available force/torque
measurements. The grasp--place adapter uses pose, joint positions, and gripper
width; contact execution can access the broader robot measurement interface.

These are simulated, calibrated measurements. In particular, camera
extrinsics and proprioception are supplied by the simulator adapter, and
depth is not an estimate from monocular RGB. The policy observation types
contain no object pose labels, segmentation IDs, BDDL predicates, reward,
or task-success flag. Task identifiers and initial-state indices remain with
the evaluator; reset passes the instruction and a nonsemantic episode token.
This is a software interface property supported by code inspection, not a
claim of adversarial isolation.

Offline information is represented by a prior collection $\mathcal{P}$.
It includes object texture histograms, dimensions derived from public
collision geometry, robot geometry, class-specific handling rules, and
motion thresholds. The evaluated controller is therefore
$a_t=\pi(o_{\leq t},\ell;\mathcal{P})$, where $\ell$ is the instruction.
No action-policy fitting or demonstration loading occurs in this evaluation.
The detector's pretraining and the controller's manual development remain
part of the system's knowledge.

\subsection{Constrained Language Compilation}
The compiler maps supported language to
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{G}(\ell)=(s_1,\ldots,s_M),\qquad
 s_j=(k_j,e_j^{\rm src},e_j^{\rm dst},\rho_j).
\end{equation}
Here $k_j$ is a skill, the entity bindings specify source and destination,
and $\rho_j$ represents a placement relation or instance selector.
The vocabulary contains pick, place-on, place-in, relative placement,
stack, open, close, push, turn-on, and turn-off.
For example, a two-object basket instruction becomes two successive
pick/place pairs. A drawer instruction can produce an articulation segment
followed by a manipulation segment.

Bindings retain relations such as \emph{next to}, \emph{between}, or an ordering
among visible instances. They do not encode the simulator's object identity.
The complete program undergoes capability checks before motion, including
valid skill arguments and consistency of held-object state. Compilation
success only establishes representational support; it does not establish
physical executability. The deployed grammar is tailored to known LIBERO
phrases and an allow-listed entity vocabulary. An optional semantic-resolver
interface exists, but no language-model resolver is configured in this run.

\subsection{Visual Grounding and Geometric State}
The deployed Grounding DINO Tiny checkpoint contains
\NeuralParameters{} unique parameters, including its visual and text
encoders. All weights are frozen, and one detector instance serves both
camera views in each evaluation process. The asset gallery, language
compiler, and geometric controllers add no neural model parameters.
The pinned checkpoint and a reproducible parameter count are recorded
with the manuscript artifacts.

Each calibrated pixel $\tilde u=(u,v,1)^\top$ with valid depth contributes
\begin{equation}
 x_W=R_{WC}\big(D(u,v)K^{-1}\tilde u\big)+p_{WC}.
 \label{eq:backproject}
\end{equation}
Workspace filtering, support estimation, and connected components yield
candidate point sets. The pipeline fits observed bounds and uses
texture/size priors to classify and complete partial observations. The
gallery computes a color similarity
\begin{equation}
 b(h,h_k)=\sum_i\sqrt{h_i h_{k,i}}
\end{equation}
between normalized HSV histograms. Its score combines color and shape terms,
with configured weights $0.4$ and $0.6$, a softmax temperature of $0.13$,
and additional class-dependent footprint gates. Grounding DINO supplies
language-conditioned boxes that are converted to 3-D hypotheses using depth.
Thus a detector box is a proposal, rather than an oracle object mask.

Cross-camera hypotheses with matching labels are clustered in 3-D. For a
cluster, the implemented fusion weights are proportional to
$\max(q_i,0.05)\sqrt{n_i}$, where $q_i$ is confidence and $n_i$ is point
count. The default center-distance threshold is $4.5$\,cm. These thresholds
are empirical operating choices; nearby repeated objects can still produce
ambiguous associations.

The state retains both observed support/bounds and completed geometry.
This distinction matters when an object is partially visible: a size prior
can stabilize a center estimate, but should not be mistaken for measured
free space. Geometric selectors resolve the intended instance from the
available candidates and reference objects. At selected phase transitions,
new views are requested and checked against prior observations to reduce
hand-occlusion-induced jumps. Specialized fixture and cavity routines refine
support surfaces, opening regions, and admissible approach geometry.

\subsection{Grasping, Transport, and Placement}
Consecutive manipulation skills share a grasp--place controller. Its phases
include acquisition, pregrasp motion, approach, finger closure, retention
checking, lift, transport, descent, release, and retreat. Transitions combine
pose tolerances, measured gripper width, visual evidence, and bounded retry
logic. Different grasp modes account for exterior pinch, rim pinch, and
interior expansion; mode choice depends on the object and observed
accessibility. These are mechanical templates with specialized branches.

For target pose $(p^\star,R^\star)$, the basic servo emits
\begin{equation}
 a_t=\operatorname{clip}_{[-1,1]}
 \begin{bmatrix}
 (p^\star-p_t)/s_p\\
 \operatorname{Log}(R^\star R_t^\top)^\vee/s_R\\
 g_t
 \end{bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:servo}
\end{equation}
The general grasp--place defaults are $s_p=0.05$\,m and
$s_R=0.25$\,rad; cavity handling can select another rotation scale.
The nominal pose tolerances are $8$\,mm and $0.12$\,rad. These are
normalization scales and transition tolerances, not measured tracking-error
bounds or a continuous-time stability guarantee.

The gripper and carried object generally have different centers.
Let $\hat d_t$ be the estimated world-frame vector from the end effector to
the carried-object reference point. For desired object location
$p_o^\star$, placement planning uses the geometric relation
\begin{equation}
 p_{\rm ee}^\star=p_o^\star-\hat d^\star,\qquad
 \hat d^\star=\Delta R\,\hat d_t
 \label{eq:offset}
\end{equation}
when the planned wrist motion rotates the held geometry by $\Delta R$.
The implementation adds support, release-height, aperture, and
fixture-specific corrections to this relation. Offset estimates may come
from precontact geometry or refreshed RGB-D evidence; their origin is retained
in the execution trace. This allows inspection of placement decisions without
assuming that all offset estimates are equally reliable.

Release is followed by sensor-based verification. A local geometric check can
reject a placement even when the benchmark predicate has already been true,
or accept a relation that the benchmark does not recognize. Section
\ref{sec:outcomes} quantifies this distinction rather than using controller
completion as the reported success metric.

\subsection{Contact Skills and Sequential Execution}
The dispatcher separates consecutive grasp--place steps from atomic contact
skills. Drawer and door interactions use observed fixture geometry to
construct approach, engagement, motion, release, and retreat stages.
Knob and push skills use their own target detectors and motion templates.
Shared episode state includes an initial robot-pose reference and, where
applicable, an observed drawer anchor for subsequent placement.

Contact evidence combines position error, lack of measured progress, and
changes in end-effector force. One implemented guard requires a minimum
contact duration and bounded position error, together with either a stalled
error reduction or sufficient force change. Such a guard indicates a
candidate contact condition; it is not the task predicate. Specialized
post-contact visual checks assess the resulting fixture configuration.

Local recovery is bounded by phase counters and geometric checks.
Examples include releasing a rejected grasp, reacquiring a visible handle,
or performing a short clearance motion after stalled drawer interaction.
These routines preserve the distinction between a robot-side mechanical
signal and an estimate of task completion. Their hand-selected parameters
are part of the fixed controller configuration evaluated below.

Several fixture skills refine the grounded object box into physical contact
geometry. A complete visible burner disk anchors the stove support centre;
public plate thickness supplies the offset from the visible disk to the
load-bearing surface. That unoccluded estimate is retained across sequential
placements. For an open microwave, visible vertical control-panel and side-wall
surfaces, together with the roof height, constrain the cavity and hinge frame
through public physical dimensions. A flat tabletop crop or a roof without
supporting vertical structure is insufficient for this fit.

Placement also accounts for the hand's withdrawal. The microwave approach
aligns the payload with the cavity width and uses a lateral entry path.
Repeated basket placements receive separate diagonal landing centres.
Before withdrawing from a basket, the controller opens the jaws to a measured
clearance; a bounded open-wrist tilt clears tall bodies near the palm.
All such proposals use language, RGB-D estimates, and robot measurements;
the external task predicate is unavailable to their selection or execution.

Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long, ten tasks each, and official initial-state

starting at a later initial-state index, because resetting can affect fixture
configuration beyond the saved joint state.

pose control. A ten-step settling period precedes scored actions. The

\subsection{External Scoring and Internal Completion}
Let $q_t$ be the official success predicate observed by the evaluator.
With policy-controlled stopping time $\tau$ capped at budget $H$, the
recorded outcome is
\begin{equation}
 Y=\bigvee_{t=1}^{\tau}q_t\ \lor\ q_{\rm final},
 \qquad \tau\leq H.
 \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
The final query also handles a zero-action stop. Reward and success do not
enter the policy observation. The evaluator continues after a success signal
until the controller requests stopping or the action budget expires.
The score therefore represents \emph{ever reaching} the predicate during
this rollout, not necessarily satisfying it at the final frame.

Each record additionally stores terminal controller state, step count,
elapsed time, failure message, and geometric traces. The reported success rate
is $\widehat p=N^{-1}\sum_iY_i$. Equal task sample counts make the overall
episode average equal to the macro-average over tasks in the completed run.
Each reported batch retains all planned failures; candidate outcomes are not
mixed within a task. Controller-internal retries remain within one episode.

\subsection{Controller Completion Is a Different Outcome}

\DisagreementCount{}/\EpisodeCount{} (\DisagreementRate{}\%).
The latter group includes cases where a predicate is reached before a later
local failure or budget exhaustion. Because the recorder retains an
ever-success latch, these records do not imply successful final-state
verification.

Using local completion as the benchmark label would change the measured
outcome; feeding external success to the controller would change its
information interface. We record both values separately.
The records lack first-success time and a separate final-only label, so they
cannot quantify changes in final-state success from continued execution
or the runtime saved by an early-stop evaluator.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The evaluated system combines a small neural model, no demonstration-based
action-policy training, and inspectable execution stages. These properties
depend on calibrated RGB-D, object priors, a constrained grammar, and
manually developed skills. They describe a useful operating point for known
environments, with task performance and development effort both dependent
on the coverage of those skills.

\paragraph{Resources and development effort.}
The parameter count and weight-file size quantify the neural component.
They do not establish proportional savings in end-to-end latency or peak
memory, which also depend on perception processing, control, and simulation.
Avoiding action-policy fitting does not remove detector pretraining or the
engineering effort required to build and revise geometric skills. The
recorded stages support inspection, but debugging effort was not measured
against a learned policy.

\paragraph{Generalization and novelty.}
The run covers known assets, language forms, and one simulated embodiment
with calibrated depth. It does not evaluate transfer to unseen assets,
language, perturbed calibration, or physical hardware, and provides no
held-out history of controller development.
The rates characterize the deployed benchmark system.
Its geometric operations and state machines are established techniques;
the contribution is their documented integration and empirical
characterization.

\paragraph{Reliability and interpretation.}
Bounded actions and local guards limit the implemented commands, but do not
establish collision safety, contact-force safety, or real-world reliability.
The narrow ten-trial task samples can also conceal rare failure modes.
The observed controller/evaluator disagreement points to a specific
measurement improvement: record first-success time and final predicate
separately, then evaluate alternative stopping conventions without silently
changing the policy's observations.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
Source and resource checksums, fixed initial-state identifiers, and
record-derived figures support reconstruction of this evaluation.
Boundary counters are implementation-generated records, not independent
attestations.
